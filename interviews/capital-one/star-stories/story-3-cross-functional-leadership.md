# STAR Story 3 — Cross-Functional Leadership (Internal Developer Portal)

**Maps to:** Work Together, Excellence

## Full Answer

> **Situation:** Developers were spending 30-50% of their time on infrastructure setup instead of building product — every new service meant re-solving the same setup problems.
>
> **Task:** I led the frontend architecture for a unified internal developer portal meant to fix that.
>
> **Action:** This required partnering across 8+ teams spanning platform, security, and product — each with its own opinion on what the portal should do. I designed a reusable module system so teams could compose what they needed instead of everyone building bespoke UI, and I pushed back explicitly on scope creep when individual teams wanted one-off customizations that would have undermined the "golden path" the whole portal was meant to provide. Getting to consensus on what "golden path" even meant across 8 teams was harder than any of the engineering.
>
> **Result:** 20-40% reduction in repetitive work across teams, 30-70% faster onboarding for new engineers, and service creation dropped from weeks to minutes.

## Follow-Up Prep (draft answers — adapt with your real specifics)

**"Which team was hardest to align with?"**
> Have a specific team and a specific point of disagreement ready — likely a team with the most unusual/legacy workflow, since they had the most to lose from standardization. The strong version of this answer names the actual disagreement (not just "they were resistant") and how it got resolved — did they get an exception, did the module system flex to cover their case, or did they eventually adopt the standard path?

**"How did you handle the team that wanted bespoke UI?"**
> This is the scope-creep pushback mentioned in the Action step — the answer should show you didn't just say no. Likely pattern: understand *why* they wanted something bespoke (usually a legitimate underlying need), then either extend the reusable module system to cover that legitimate need generically (so the next team with a similar need doesn't need a one-off either), or make an explicit, time-boxed exception rather than an open-ended fork. The lesson to land: "no" to the specific request, "yes" to the underlying need, solved generically.
