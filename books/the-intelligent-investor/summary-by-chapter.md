# The Intelligent Investor - Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

## Preface by Warren E. Buffett

**Key Points:**
- Graham's book is "by far the best book about investing ever written"
- Successful investing requires:
  - Sound intellectual framework for decisions (the book provides)
  - Emotional discipline (you must supply)
- Does not require stratospheric IQ or inside information
- Pay special attention to Chapters 8 and 20
- "Follow Graham and you will profit from folly rather than participate in it"

## A Note About Benjamin Graham by Jason Zweig

**Who Was Graham:**
- Born May 9, 1894, in London as Benjamin Grossbaum
- Family moved to NYC, fell into poverty after father's death (1903)
- Mother lost money in 1907 crash - formative experience
- Brilliant student at Columbia, graduated 1914 (second in class)
- Started on Wall Street as clerk, eventually ran own investment partnership

**His Track Record:**
- Graham-Newman Corp: 14.7% annually (1936-1956) vs. 12.2% for market
- Survived 70% decline in 1929-1932
- Pioneered "security analysis" - researching stocks in microscopic detail
- Famous example: Northern Pipe Line (bought at $65, worth $80 in bonds alone)

**Core Principles Established:**
1. Stock = ownership interest in actual business with underlying value independent of price
2. Market is a pendulum between unsustainable optimism and unjustified pessimism
3. Future value is function of present price (higher price = lower return)
4. Margin of safety is only true protection against errors
5. Secret to success is inside yourself - discipline and courage matter most

**Book's Value**: Applies Graham's timeless ideas to today's markets while keeping text intact, with modern commentary showing principles remain relevant.

## Introduction: What This Book Expects to Accomplish

**Purpose**: Provide guidance on investment policy for laymen, focusing on principles and attitudes rather than technical security analysis.

**Historical Perspective**:
- Uses data going back 100 years (to 1871)
- Santayana's warning: "Those who do not remember the past are condemned to repeat it"
- Not a "how to make a million" book

**Example - The Raskob Folly (1929)**:
- John J. Raskob article: "Everybody Ought to Be Rich"
- Promised $80,000 from $15/month investment over 20 years
- Reality: Only achieved $8,500 (1929-1948) - but still 8% compounded
- Demonstrates danger of unrealistic expectations

**Investment vs. Trading**:
- Book not for speculators or market timers
- "Following the market" (buy high, sell low) never works long-term
- Graham knew NO ONE who made lasting money this way

**Key Changes Since 1965** (writing in 1972):
1. Unprecedented rise in interest rates on high-grade bonds
2. 35% fall in leading stock prices (1969-70)
3. Persistent inflation despite business decline
4. Rise of conglomerates and franchise operations
5. Penn Central bankruptcy and debt problems
6. "Performance" vogue in fund management

**Core Policy Recommendations**:
- Defensive investor: at least 25% in stocks and bonds each, up to 75%
- Standard split: 50-50 between stocks and bonds
- Adjust based on market conditions, but within limits
- Bonds now yielding more than stocks (historic reversal)

**Two Types of Investors**:
- **Defensive (Passive)**: Avoids serious mistakes, wants freedom from effort
- **Enterprising (Active)**: Willing to devote time and skill to selection

**Important Warnings**:
1. Obvious industry growth ≠ obvious investor profits (airline example)
2. Experts can't reliably pick winning industries/companies
3. "What Works" formulas eventually fail
4. Psychology and valuation habits matter more than stock-picking ability

**Book's Objectives**:
- Guide against substantial error areas
- Develop comfortable, sustainable policies
- Emphasize investor psychology and discipline
- Implant habit of measuring/quantifying (comparing price to value)
- Help establish proper mental and emotional attitudes

**Closing Wisdom**: "Sound investment principles produced generally sound results" through past century's vicissitudes - must assume they will continue to do so.

## Chapter 1: Investment versus Speculation

**Graham's Definition**:
"An investment operation is one which, upon thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and an adequate return. Operations not meeting these requirements are speculative."

**Historical Context**:
- After 1929-1932, ALL common stocks viewed as speculative
- By 1972, everybody in market called "investor" regardless of behavior
- Confusion between terms increased with market advances

**The Critical Distinction**:
- Has "all but forgotten" quality despite remaining crucial
- Wall Street should reinstate and emphasize it
- Failure to do so leads to speculative losses without proper warnings

**On Speculation Itself**:
- Neither illegal, immoral, nor necessarily unprofitable
- Some speculation is necessary and unavoidable in markets
- Can be intelligent if properly approached

**Three Forms of Unintelligent Speculation**:
1. Speculating when you think you're investing
2. Speculating seriously without proper knowledge/skill
3. Risking more money than you can afford to lose

**Margin Trading**:
- Non-professionals operating on margin are *ipso facto* speculating
- Broker has duty to advise them of this
- "Hot" stock issues are speculation or gambling

**Speculation Guidelines**:
- Put aside small portion of capital in separate account
- Never add more just because market is up
- Never mingle speculative and investment accounts
- Think of it as limited fun, not wealth-building

**Results for Defensive Investor**:

*1964 Recommendations* (DJIA at 874):
- 50-50 split between bonds and stocks
- Bond yields: 4.5%, Stock yields: 3.2%
- Expected stock component: 3.5-4.5% dividend + 3.5-4% appreciation = 7.5-8.5%
- Combined portfolio: ~6% before taxes

*1972 Analysis* (DJIA at 900):
- Bond yields jumped to 7.5%, Stock yields remained 3.5%
- Historic reversal: bonds now yield more than stocks
- Expected stocks: 3.5% dividend + 4% appreciation = 7.5%
- After-tax: stocks ~5.3%, similar to tax-free bonds

**Three Investment Possibilities Graham Considered**:
1. Accelerating inflation (stocks preferable)
2. American business becomes more profitable (stocks justified)
3. Another speculative rise without justification (likely regret follows)

**Portfolio Policy Recommendations** (1972):

For bonds:
- U.S. Savings Bonds (Series E and H)
- Direct U.S. government obligations
- Tax-free state and municipal bonds
- Corporate bonds (highest quality)

For stocks:
- Keep 25-75% in stocks
- 50-50 split as standard
- Adjust based on valuation, not predictions
- Include leading issues only, well-diversified

**Formula Approach**:
- Maintain roughly equal division between bonds and stocks
- Rebalance when one component reaches ~55% (sell 1/11 and transfer to other)
- Simple, moves in right direction, restrains dangerous behavior
- Yale University used similar formula successfully (35% normal holding)

**Results for Enterprising Investor**:

**Market Timing (Formula Investing)**:
- Used to work well pre-1949
- Since then, less reliable due to changed market patterns
- Requires special "feel" beyond intelligence
- Not recommended as primary strategy

**Other Approaches**:
1. **Short-term selectivity**: Buying based on recent/expected earnings
   - Problem: Wall Street already knows and has priced this in
   - You're competing with professionals at their game

2. **Long-term selectivity**: Growth stocks
   - Problem: Predicting long-term future extremely difficult
   - Airlines example: Great growth, terrible investor returns

**Graham's Key Conclusion**:
To get better than average results, investor must follow policies that are:
1. Inherently sound and promising
2. Not popular on Wall Street

**Why This Can Work**:
- Speculative movements carried too far in both directions
- Stocks can be undervalued due to lack of interest
- "Most trading participants don't appear to know one part of their anatomy from another"
- Many discrepancies exist between price and value

**The Challenge**:
- Buying neglected undervalued issues is "protracted and patience-trying"
- Shorting overvalued popular issues tests courage and capital
- "Principle is sound, application is not impossible, but not easy"

**Special Situations**:
- Arbitrages, liquidations, mergers, protected hedges
- Used to provide 20%+ returns with minimum risk
- Became less reliable as more competition entered field

**Historical Success Examples**:
1. Formula based on earnings and interest rates (worked 1897-1949, not after)
2. Dow Theory (good 1897-1933, questionable since)
3. Bargain issues selling below working capital (profitable when available)

**Graham's 1970 Observations**:
- "Sub-working-capital" issues appeared again at market lows
- Opportunities exist for those with patience and discipline
- Should add ~5% annually to stock portion returns if properly executed

## Commentary on Chapter 1 (Jason Zweig)

**Main Theme**: Wall Street vs. Main Street - Brokers profit when you trade, regardless of your results.

**The Three Essential Elements of Investing**:
1. Thoroughly analyze a company and soundness of underlying businesses
2. Deliberately protect against serious losses
3. Aspire to "adequate," not extraordinary, performance

**Investors vs. Speculators**:
- **Investor**: Calculates what stock is worth based on business value
- **Speculator**: Gambles on price movements based on what others will pay
- Graham: "investors judge the market price by established standards of value"
- Speculators: "base standards of value upon the market price"

**The Casino Analogy**:
- On Wall Street, odds calibrated so house (brokers) always win
- Day traders make money for brokers, not themselves
- Investing is unique casino where you CAN'T lose if you play by right rules

**The Speculation Nation (1990s)**:
- America transformed into culture of rapid trading
- People believed anything that "worked" was valid
- Temporarily high returns don't prove a method safe or sustainable
- Like driving 130 mph - might work once, but eventually kills you

**Speed Comparison**:
- 1973: NYSE turnover 20% (stocks held 5 years average)
- 2002: NYSE turnover 105% (held 11.4 months)
- Mutual fund holding periods dropped from 3 years to 10.9 months
- Even Fidelity Magellan's Vinik sold $19B in 8 weeks after claiming "long-term approach"

**NASDAQ Velocity** (1999):
- Some stocks like Puma Technology changed hands every 5.7 days
- "Stock Market for the Next Hundred Years" but owners couldn't hold 100 hours

**Online Trading Propaganda**:
- Discover Brokerage ad: Tow-truck driver owns island from trading
- Ameritrade: Housewife makes $1,700 clicking mouse
- Waterhouse: Phil Jackson "I'm going to make it [trade] right now"
- Created illusion trading requires no thought or work

**Day Trading Phenomenon**:
- 6 million trading online by 1999
- ~600,000 day trading
- Barbra Streisand: "I'm Taurus the bull, so I react to red. If I see red, I sell"
- Nicholas Birbas (former waiter): Traded 10x daily, expected $100K/year

**The Problem with "Hot Stocks"**:
- Stocks became abstract blips, decoupled from underlying companies
- Juno Online (1999): Announced plan to LOSE money on purpose - stock tripled
- People bought tickers (CBLT, INKT, PCLN) without knowing the businesses
- Temco Services mistaken for Ticketmaster - tripled on ticker confusion alone

**Analyst Madness**:
- Henry Blodget: Amazon "clearly more art than science" - raised target to $400
- Walter Piecyk: Qualcomm will hit $1,000 (it was already up 1,842% that year)
- Both stocks subsequently crashed

**Failed Formulas**:

*The January Effect*:
- Small stocks rally in January due to tax-loss selling
- Worked 8.5% outperformance (1962-1979)
- Weakened as more knew about it (4.4% in 1980s, 5.8% in 1990s)
- Transaction costs eat up all gains for individuals

*What Works on Wall Street*:
- James O'Shaughnessy's mechanical formula
- Claimed could turn $10K into $8 million (1954-1994)
- Launched mutual funds based on findings
- Funds immediately underperformed market for 4 years
- O'Shaughnessy abandoned strategy, leaving investors stranded

*The Foolish Four*:
- Motley Fool website promoted "15 minutes per year" formula
- Pick 5 Dow stocks with lowest prices and highest yields
- Drop the best one, overweight second-best
- Claimed "minimal risk" and "crush your mutual funds"
- Made no logical sense (why drop the best?)
- Lost 14% in 2000 while Dow lost only 4.7%

**Graham's Law**: "A kind of self-destructive process—akin to the law of diminishing returns"
- If formula is random luck, time exposes it
- If formula truly worked, publicity destroys it
- Either way, returns fade

**Three Rules for Speculation**:
1. Never delude yourself into thinking speculation is investing
2. Never take it seriously - it becomes mortally dangerous
3. Put strict limits on speculation (max 10% of portfolio)

**The "Mad Money" Account**:
- Keep separate from investment accounts
- Never mingle the two (physically or mentally)
- Never exceed 10% of assets
- This confines and restrains gambling instinct
- Prevents fooling yourself about what you're doing

**Bottom Line**: "Don't just do something, stand there." Long-term investor is the only kind that succeeds. Active trading is financial suicide dressed up as opportunity.

## Chapter 2: The Investor and Inflation

**The Inflation Argument for Stocks**:
- Fixed-dollar assets (bonds, cash) lose purchasing power in inflation
- Stocks offer potential offset through dividend increases and price appreciation
- Some advisors recommended 100% stocks for all investors
- Graham's view: This advice ignores valuation and goes too far

**Intelligent Response**:
Even high-quality stocks can't be better than bonds at ANY price regardless of:
- How high the stock market is
- How low dividend return is compared to bond yields

**Historical Inflation Data** (1915-1970):

Largest five-year inflation: 1915-1920 (prices nearly doubled)
- Compare to 15% inflation 1965-1970
- Multiple periods of both inflation and deflation
- No consistent pattern

**Recent Pattern** (1950-1970):
- Average annual inflation: 2.5%
- 1965-1970: 4.5%
- 1970 alone: 5.4%
- Graham expected future rate ~3% annually

**Impact of 3% Inflation**:
- Would consume ~half of bond income (after taxes)
- Serious but not devastating if investor spent only half the interest
- Maintains purchasing power even with 3% inflation

**Stock Performance Analysis**:

*Long-term Record* (1915-1970):
- DJIA rose from 77 to 753 (~4% annual compound)
- Plus ~4% dividend yield
- Total: ~8% annual return
- Better than bonds over 55 years

*But Questions Remain*:
- Will stocks continue outperforming at same rate?
- Graham's answer: Flat NO
- May do better, but far from certain

**The Time Element Problem**:
Two different timeframes matter:
1. Long-term future (25+ years)
2. Short/intermediate term (5 years or less) - determines investor's psychology and decisions

**Key Finding**:
"No close time connection between inflationary/deflationary conditions and stock earnings/prices"

**Evidence - 1966-1970**:
- Cost of living rose 22% (largest in five years since 1946-1950)
- Yet stock earnings AND prices both DECLINED
- Contradicts theory that inflation helps stocks

**Corporate Earnings Analysis**:

Historical data shows:
- Corporate profit rate on capital has FALLEN despite inflation
- Earnings didn't increase just because prices rose
- All gains in DJIA earnings came from reinvested profits, not inflation benefit

*Why Inflation Didn't Help*:
1. Wage rates rose faster than productivity gains
2. Need for huge new capital held down sales/capital ratios

**The Debt Problem**:

Table 2-2 shows (1950-1969):
- Corporate debt expanded nearly 5-fold
- Profits before taxes only slightly more than doubled
- Interest rates rose dramatically
- By 1969, net earnings were only 13.2% of debt (vs. 30% in 1950)

**Conclusion**:
- Part of 11% earnings on equity achieved by using large amounts of new debt at 4% after-tax
- Without increased debt, earnings rate would have been even lower
- Inflation actually HURT rather than helped corporations

**Alternatives to Stocks for Inflation Hedging**:

*Gold*:
- Standard worldwide hedge historically
- Illegal for Americans (1935-1971)
- Rose only 35% (35 years): from $35 to $48 per ounce
- No income, plus storage costs
- Would have done much better in savings account

*Other "Things"*:
- Art, diamonds, rare books, stamps, coins
- Many show striking advances
- But prices often "artificial or precarious or unreal"
- Example: Silver dollar dated 1804 (not even minted that year) at $67,500
- Graham admits being "out of depth" in this area

*Real Estate*:
- Traditionally sound long-term investment
- Subject to wide fluctuations
- Errors possible in location, price
- Pitfalls in salesmen's wiles
- Difficult to diversify for modest means
- "Be sure it's yours before you go into it"

**Final Conclusion**:
Despite uncertainties, return to recommended policy:
- Don't put all funds in one basket (all bonds OR all stocks)
- Maintain significant portions in both
- More dependent on portfolio = more necessary to guard against unexpected
- Stock component provides inflation insurance
- Bond component provides safety and income

**Quote from 1965 Edition** (Repeated in 1972):
"We have no enthusiasm for common stocks at these levels (892 for DJIA). For reasons already given we feel that the defensive investor cannot afford to be without an appreciable proportion of common stocks, even if we regard them as the lesser of two evils—the greater being the risks in an all-bond holding."

## Commentary on Chapter 2 (Jason Zweig)

**Current Environment** (2003):
- Inflation averaged less than 2.2% (1997-2002)
- Economists believe even this is overstated
- Many pundits declared "inflation is dead"
- True rate probably around 1% annually

**The Money Illusion**:
Psychologists' term for tendency to focus on nominal (absolute) changes rather than real (after-inflation) changes:
- 2% raise with 4% inflation FEELS better than 2% cut with 0% inflation
- Yet both leave you 2% worse off after inflation
- We view positive nominal change as good even if real result is negative
- Example: Happy earning 11% on CDs in 1980 (losing money after inflation), disappointed earning 2% in 2003 (keeping pace with inflation)

**Why Inflation Still Matters**:

Three reasons to remain vigilant despite low current inflation:

1. **Recent History (1973-1982)**:
   - Inflation averaged 8.6% annually
   - Prices more than doubled in decade
   - Inflation hit 13.3% in 1979 alone ("stagflation")
   - $100 in 1973 required $230 by 1982
   - Dollar value shriveled to less than 45 cents

2. **Global Evidence**:
   - Since 1960, 69% of market-oriented countries had at least one year of 25%+ inflation
   - These periods destroyed average of 53% of purchasing power
   - Would be "crazy" to think America is permanently exempt

3. **Government Incentives**:
   - Rising prices allow government to pay debts with cheapened dollars
   - Completely eradicating inflation runs against economic self-interest of any borrowing government

**Historical U.S. Inflation**:
- American Revolution: Prices tripled annually 1777-1779
- Civil War: 29% annual (North), 200% annual (Confederacy)
- 1946: 18.1% inflation
- Not unprecedented in American history

**How Well Do Stocks Hedge Against Inflation?**

Figure 2-1 (1926-2002 data) reveals surprising truth:
- When prices fell (deflation), stock returns were terrible (lost up to 43%)
- When inflation exceeded 6%, stocks also performed poorly
- Lost money in 8 of 14 years when inflation > 6%
- Average return only 2.6% in those high-inflation years
- Mild inflation allows companies to pass costs to customers (good)
- High inflation wreaks havoc (bad)

**Success Rate**:
- Stocks outpaced inflation in 50 of 64 five-year periods (78% of time)
- But that means 22% of time stocks FAILED to keep up with inflation
- Impressive but imperfect as inflation hedge

**Two New Inflation Fighters**:

### TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities):
*Features*:
- U.S. government bonds that automatically rise with inflation
- First issued 1997
- Safe from default risk (backed by U.S. government)
- Guarantee value won't be eroded by inflation
- Package both financial safety and purchasing power protection

*Tax Complication*:
- When value rises with inflation, IRS considers this taxable income
- Even though it's only a paper gain
- Best held in tax-deferred accounts (IRA, Keogh, 401(k))

*Where to Buy*:
- Directly from government at www.publicdebt.treas.gov
- Through low-cost mutual funds (Vanguard Inflation-Protected Securities, Fidelity Inflation-Protected Bond Fund)
- Don't trade them - hold permanently
- Allocate at least 10% of retirement assets to TIPS

*Benefits*:
- Absolutely safe portion of money
- Entirely beyond reach of inflation
- Ideal substitute for cash portion of retirement funds

### REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts):
*What They Are*:
- Companies that own and collect rent from commercial and residential properties
- Available bundled into real-estate mutual funds
- Do decent job combating inflation

*Recommended Funds*:
- Vanguard REIT Index Fund (best choice)
- Cohen & Steers Realty Shares
- Columbia Real Estate Equity Fund
- Fidelity Real Estate Investment Fund

*Limitations*:
- Unlikely to be foolproof inflation-fighter
- Should give some defense against erosion of purchasing power
- Won't hamper overall returns
- Case is weaker if you already own home (already have real estate exposure)

**Final Wisdom**:
Defensive investor must branch out beyond stocks for complete protection against inflation. Combination of stocks, TIPS, and REITs provides best defense while maintaining balanced approach Graham advocates.

## Chapter 3: A Century of Stock-Market History

**Purpose**: Provide enterprising investor with adequate idea of:
- Major price level fluctuations over century
- Varying relationships between stock prices, earnings, and dividends
- Background to form worthwhile judgments about market attractiveness at different times

**Data Period**: 1871-1972 (exactly 100 years available)

**Two Objectives**:
1. Show general manner of underlying advance through many cycles
2. View in successive ten-year averages (not just prices, but earnings and dividends)

**The Nineteen Bull and Bear Market Cycles** (Table 3-1):

Uses two indexes:
1. Cowles Commission spliced to S&P 500 composite
2. Dow Jones Industrial Average (30 stocks, starting 1897)

**Three Distinct Periods** (Chart I):

*First Pattern (1900-1924)*:
- Series of similar market cycles (3-5 years each)
- Annual advance averaged just 3%

*Second Pattern (1924-1949)*:
- "New Era" bull market culminating 1929
- Terrible aftermath of collapse
- Irregular fluctuations until 1949
- From 1924 to 1949: only 1.5% annual advance
- Public had NO enthusiasm for stocks by 1949
- By "rule of opposites," time was ripe for greatest bull market in history

*Third Pattern (1949-1968)*:
- Greatest bull market in history
- Setbacks in 1956-57 and 1961-62 called "recessions in a single bull market"
- DJIA: 162 (mid-1949) to 995 (early 1966)
- More than sixfold advance in 17 years
- Average compounded rate: 11% per year (plus 3.5% dividends)
- S&P composite: even better performance (14 to 96)

**The Inflection Point** (1968)**:
- December 1968: S&P industrials hit 118 (108 for composite)
- Perhaps culmination of great bull market
- 36% decline 1968-1970 (DJIA 37%)
- Largest since 44% drop 1939-1942 (reflecting Pearl Harbor uncertainties)

**Post-1970 Recovery**:
- Massive and speedy recovery from May 1970 low
- New all-time high for S&P industrials early 1972
- 1949-1970: ~9% annual advance (using average figures both years)
- Much greater than any similar period before 1950
- BUT last decade (1961-1970): only 5.25% for S&P, 3% for DJIA

**Ten-Year Average Analysis** (Table 3-2):

**Smoothed Data Shows**:
- Persistent growth overall
- Only two decades showed decreases in earnings and prices (1891-1900, 1931-1940)
- No decade after 1900 showed decrease in average dividends
- Rates of growth vary considerably
- Post-WWII performance superior to earlier decades
- 1960s advance less pronounced than 1950s

**Important Features**:

*1970 Deterioration*:
- Overall earnings rate on capital fell to lowest since WWII
- Many companies reported losses
- First time in three decades with numerous important bankruptcies
- Suggests "great boom era may have ended in 1969-70"

*Price/Earnings Ratio Changes*:
- June 1949: S&P at 6.3x past 12-month earnings
- March 1961: 22.9x earnings
- Dividend yield fell from 7% (1949) to 3.0% (1961)
- Meanwhile bond yields rose from 2.60% to 4.50%
- "Most remarkable turnabout in public attitude in all stock-market history"

*Long Experience Showed Fears Unfounded*:
- 1962 fears of repeat 1929 not confirmed
- Market continued upward despite high valuations
- DJIA closing 1970 same as 6.5 years earlier
- "Soaring Sixties" proved mostly a march up hills and down again
- Nothing comparable to 1929-1932 disaster

**Stock Market Level Analysis** (Early 1972):

**Previous Edition Conclusions Review**:

*1948 (DJIA 180)*:
- "Not too high in relation to underlying values"
- Correct in hindsight

*1953 (DJIA 275)*:
- 50%+ gain in five years
- Difficult to reach definitive conclusion
- Said "from value standpoint...conclusion must be favorable"
- But concerned about length of advance and high absolute level
- Advised caution/compromise
- Not particularly brilliant counsel (market rose another 100% in 5 years)

*1959 (DJIA 584)*:
- "Present level of stock prices is dangerous"
- "Market momentum will inevitably carry it to unjustifiable heights"
- "Cannot imagine market without serious losses"
- Somewhat vindicated: Rose to 685 (1961), fell to 536 (May 1962)
- 27% loss in six months
- IBM dropped from 607 to 300
- Debacle in "hot issues" - many lost 90%+

*1964 (DJIA 892)*:
- "Old standards appear inapplicable; new standards not tested by time"
- Predicted either 50% advance to 1350 or collapse to 450
- "If 1964 level not too high, how could we say ANY price level is too high?"
- Advised: No borrowing, no increase in stock proportion, reduce to max 50%
- Vindicated: advanced 11% to 995, then fell to 632 (1970)
- AAA Enterprises example (Chapter 17): sold at $14, hit 28, fell to $0.25

**What We Learn**:
- All judgments could be defended "by adroit arguments"
- But probably less useful than pedestrian counsels
- Consistent controlled policy beats market timing attempts

**1972 Analysis** (Table 3-3):

**Data Comparison** (Year-ends 1948, 1953, 1958, 1963, 1968 + October 1971):

Using S&P composite at 100 (various times in 1971, early 1972):

*Price/Earnings Ratios*:
- 3-year P/E lower in 1971 than 1963 and 1968
- About same as 1958
- Much higher than early years of bull market

*Bond Yield Comparison*:
- Ratio of stock returns to bond returns worsened throughout entire period
- January 1972 least favorable to stocks vs. bonds of any period examined
- Dividend yields completely reversed from bond yields
- 1948: Stocks yielded 2x bonds
- 1972: Bonds yielded 2x+ stocks

**Graham's Final Judgment on 1972 Market**:
"The adverse change in bond-yield/stock-yield ratio fully offsets better P/E ratio based on 3-year earnings. View of early 1972 market level tends to be same as 7 years ago—unattractive from standpoint of conservative investment. (Applies to DJIA range 800-950)"

**Technical Outlook**:
- Pattern suggests irregular recovery from 1969-70 setback
- Historically, such recoveries usher in new bull market stage
- Wall Street expected this in 1971
- No "new-issue merry-go-round" sign (too early after 1968-70 disaster)
- Should favor another substantial rise beyond 900 before next serious setback

**But Graham's Concern**:
"Early-1971 market's disregard of harrowing experiences less than year before is disquieting. Can such heedlessness go unpunished?"

**Graham's Expectation**:
Investor must prepare for difficult times ahead in either:
1. Fairly quick replay of 1969-70 decline, OR
2. Another bull-market fling followed by more catastrophic collapse

**Course to Follow** (repeated from 1964):
Same view at 900 DJIA in early 1972 as late 1964:
1. No borrowing to buy or hold securities
2. No increase in proportion held in common stocks
3. Reduce common stocks to maximum 50% if needed
4. Pay capital-gains tax and reinvest in first-quality bonds or savings

*Graham's Logic*:
- Value of any investment is function of price paid
- Late 1990s: inflation low, profits booming, peace worldwide

*Michael Jordan Example*:
- Perhaps greatest basketball player ever
- Bulls got bargain paying $34 million/year
- But wouldn't have been justified paying $340M, $3.4B, or $34B

**The Limits of Optimism**:

*Investor Surveys*:
- Mid-1998: Expected 13% portfolio return next year

*Professional "Sophisticates"*:
- 2001: SBC Communications raised pension return assumption from 8.5% to 9.5%

- Minimum $32 billion cost to S&P 500 companies (2002-2004)

- More enthusiastic investors become in long run, more certain to be wrong in short run

*The Damage*:
- March 24, 2000: Total U.S. stock market worth $14.75 trillion
- October 9, 2002: Worth $7.34 trillion (down 50.2%)
- Loss of $7.41 trillion in just 30 months

**What's Next?**

*Graham's Method for Future Returns*:

Stock market performance depends on three factors:
1. Real growth (earnings and dividends)
2. Inflationary growth (general price rise)
3. Speculative growth/decline (public's appetite for stocks)

*Historical Data*:
- Corporate earnings growth: 1.5-2% annually (not counting inflation)
- Early 2003 inflation: 2.4% annually
- Dividend yield: 1.9%

*The Math*:
1.5-2% + 2.4% + 1.9% = 5.8-6.3%

Expected long-term return: ~6% (or 4% after inflation)

*Important Qualifiers*:
- If public gets greedy: temporarily higher returns
- If public fearful (as in 2003): temporarily lower returns
- Where we are in 2003: latter category

**Shiller's Valuation Approach**:

*Method*:
- Finance professor at Yale, inspired by Graham
- Compares S&P 500 price to average real corporate profits over past 10 years

- Early 2003: Ratio at 22.8 (still in danger zone)

Would split difference between lowest and highest past returns:
- Project ~6% annually over next decade
- Or 4% after inflation
- Matches estimate from earlier calculation
- "Whisker better" than bonds - reason to hang on to stocks

**Final Lesson**:

*Only Indisputable Truth*:
The future will always surprise us - ALWAYS!

*Corollary*:
Markets will most brutally surprise those most certain their views are right

*Graham's Wisdom*:
Stay humble about forecasting powers
- Prevents risking too much on views that may be wrong
- Lower expectations but don't depress spirit

## Chapter 4: General Portfolio Policy: The Defensive Investor

**Basic Portfolio Characteristics**:
Determined by:
- Position and characteristics of owner
- One extreme: Savings banks, insurance companies, legal trust funds (limited to high-grade bonds/stocks)
- Other extreme: Well-to-do experienced businessmen (include any securities)

**Graham's Different View on Risk/Return**:

Traditional: Return proportionate to risk willing to take
Graham's Thesis:
- Return should depend on **intelligent effort** investor willing/able to bring
- Minimum return: Passive investor (safety + freedom from concern)
- Maximum return: Alert, enterprising investor (maximum intelligence and skill)

*1965 Statement*:
"May be less real risk in buying 'bargain issue' offering large profit than conventional bond yielding 4.5%"
- More true than suspected
- Even best long-term bonds lost substantial value due to interest rate rises

**Basic Bond-Stock Allocation Problem**:

**Fundamental Rule**:
- Never less than 25% or more than 75% in common stocks
- Inverse range for bonds (75-25%)
- Implication: Standard division should be 50-50

**Traditional Rationale for Adjustment**:
- Increase stocks at "bargain price" levels in bear market
- Reduce stocks when market "dangerously high"

**The Challenge**:
"Almost a contradiction in terms to suggest average stockholder lighten holdings when market advances and add after decline"
- Goes against human nature (produces market excesses)
- Average person operates in opposite fashion

**Professional Record**:
- Mutual funds haven't followed this pattern
- "Balanced" and "common-stock" funds changed equity percentages little year-to-year
- Selling mainly to switch between holdings, not to time market

**Graham's 1972 Dilemma**:
Market has "lost contact with old bounds, new ones not yet established"
- Can't give reliable rules for 25% minimum to 75% maximum adjustments
- "Hard to see how strong confidence can be justified at levels of early 1972"

**Recommendation**: Generally not more than 50% in equities "at this time" (1972)
BUT: "Almost equally difficult to advise reduction well below 50%" unless:
- Investor is disquieted in his own mind
- Will be satisfied limiting participation in further rise to 25% of funds

**The 50-50 Formula**:

*Guiding Rule*:
Maintain as nearly as practicable equal division between bonds and stocks

*How It Works*:
- When stocks rise to 55%, sell 1/11 of stock portfolio, transfer to bonds
- When stocks fall to 45%, use 1/11 of bond fund to buy stocks

*Yale University Example*:
- Followed similar plan post-1937 with 35% "normal holding" in stocks
- Early 1950s: Gave up formula
- 1969: Held 61% in equities (71 institutions averaged 60.3%)
- Illustrates "almost lethal effect of great market advance on formula approach"

**Why Graham Still Recommends 50-50**:
1. Extremely simple
2. Aims in right direction
3. Gives follower feeling of making moves in response to market
4. Most important: **Restrains from being drawn more heavily into stocks as market rises to dangerous heights**

**Additional Benefits**:
- Truly conservative investor satisfied with gains on half portfolio in rising market
- In severe decline, derives solace from being better off than more venturesome friends

**Alternative Considerations**:

*Current Argument for Bonds* (1972):
- Much larger income return on good bonds than representative stocks
- Potent argument for favoring bond component

*Investor's Choice*:
May rest on temperament and attitude:
- Cold-blooded weigher of odds might favor 25% stocks at that time
- Wait until DJIA dividend yield was 2/3 of bond yield before 50-50 split

*Example*:
From 900 DJIA with $36 dividends:
- Would require bond yields falling from 7.5% to 5.5% (with no change in stock yields), OR
- DJIA falling to 660 (no reduction in bond yields/dividends), OR
- Combination of intermediate changes
Not especially complicated, but hard part is adopting and sticking to it

**The Bond Component**:

**Two Main Decisions**:
1. Taxable or tax-free?
2. Shorter or longer-term maturities?

**Tax Decision**:

*1972 Comparison* (20-year maturities):
- Grade Aaa corporate bonds: 7.5%
- Prime tax-free (municipals): 5.3%
- Loss of ~30% in income moving from corporate to municipal

*Rule*:
- If maximum tax bracket higher than 30%: Net saving after taxes with municipals
- Single person: 30% rate starts above $10,000 income
- Married couple: Starts above $20,000 income
- Large proportion of individuals would benefit from good municipals

**Maturity Decision**:
Involves trade-off:
- Assure against price decline by choosing shorter maturities
- At cost of: (1) Lower annual yield, (2) Loss of potential principal gain
- Graham discusses this in Chapter 8 (Market Fluctuations)

**U.S. Savings Bonds**:

*Historical Position*:
For many years, only sensible bond purchase for individuals:
- Unquestioned safety
- Higher return than other first-quality investments
- Money-back option and other privileges
- Previous edition had entire chapter: "U.S. Savings Bonds: A Boon to Investors"

*Current Status* (1972):

Still possess unique merits for any individual investor
Best choice for modest capital (not more than $10,000 in bonds)
Those with larger funds may find other mediums more desirable

**Major Bond Types for Consideration**:

### 1. U.S. Savings Bonds (Series E and Series H)

*Series H Bonds*:
- Pay interest semi-annually (like other bonds)
- Rate: 4.29% first year, then flat 5.10% for next 9 years to maturity

*Series E Bonds*:
- Interest not paid out but accrues through redemption value increase
- Sold at 75% of face value
- Mature at 100% in 5 years 10 months
- Yield: 5% compounded semi-annually if held to maturity
- If redeemed earlier: 4.01% minimum (first year) to average 5.20% (next 4-5/6 years)

*Tax Treatment*:
- Subject to Federal income tax, exempt from state income tax
- Series E: Federal tax may be paid either:
  - Annually as interest accrues, OR
  - When bond disposed of (deferral advantage)

*Unique Features*:
- Owners may cash in anytime at current redemption value (Series E)
- Series H holders may cash in at par value (cost)
- Series E exchangeable for Series H with tax advantages
- Lost, destroyed, stolen bonds replaced without cost
- Liberal provisions for co-ownership by family members

*Graham's Comment*:
No other investment combines:
1. Absolute assurance of principal and interest
2. Right to demand full "money back" anytime
3. Guarantee of at least 5% interest for at least 10 years

*Additional Benefits*:
- Earlier issues allowed extension at maturity at successively higher rates
- Deferral of income tax greatly increased effective net-after-tax rate (by ~1/3)
- Right to cash in at cost or better provided complete protection against price shrinkage
- Could benefit from interest rate rises by switching to higher-coupon issues on even-money basis

*Conclusion*:
Special advantages now will more than compensate for lower current return vs. other direct government obligations

### 2. Other United States Bonds

*Variety*:
Wide range of coupon rates and maturity dates
All completely safe for interest and principal payments

*Tax Treatment*:
- Subject to Federal income tax
- Free from state income tax

*Yields* (Late 1971):
- Long-term (10+ years): 6.09% average
- Intermediate (3-5 years): 6.35%
- Short-term: 6.03%

*Special Features*:
- 1970: Some old issues available at large discounts
- Some accepted at par for estate tax settlement
- Example: U.S. Treasury 3.5% due 1990 (sold at 60 in 1970, closed above 77 by year-end)

*Indirect Obligations*:
Often yield appreciably more than direct obligations of same maturity
Example (As of writing):
- 7.05% "Certificates Fully Guaranteed by Secretary of Transportation"
- Actually issued by Penn Central Transportation trustees
- U.S. Attorney General: Guarantee "brings into being general obligation of United States, backed by full faith and credit"
- Yielded fully 1% more than direct obligations (same 1986 maturity)

*Why the Indirection?*
- Main reason: Debt limit imposed by Congress on government borrowing
- Guarantees not regarded as debts ("semantic windfall for shrewder investors")
- Created tax-free Housing Authority bonds with equivalent of U.S. guarantee
- Also New Community Debentures

### 3. State and Municipal Bonds

*Tax Status*:
- Exempt from Federal income tax
- Usually free from income tax in state of issue (but not elsewhere)

*Types*:
- Direct obligations of state or subdivision
- Revenue bonds (dependent on receipts from toll roads, bridges, building leases, etc.)

*Quality Considerations*:
Not all tax-free bonds strong enough for defensive investor
- Should be guided by ratings from Moody's or S&P
- One of three highest ratings sufficient: Aaa (AAA), Aa (AA), or A

*Yields* (Late 1971):
- Issues in S&P municipal bond index:
  - Average AA quality rating
  - 20 years maturity
  - 5.78% yield
- Typical offering (Vineland, N.J., rated AA):
  - 3% on one-year maturity
  - 5.8% on 1995-1996 maturities

### 4. Corporation Bonds

*Tax Treatment*:
Subject to both Federal and state tax

*Yields* (Early 1972):
- Highest quality (Moody's Aaa): 7.19% for 25-year maturity
- Lower-medium-grade (Baa): 8.23% for long maturities
- Shorter terms: Somewhat less than longer-term obligations

*Comment*:
Average investor has several choices among high-grade bonds:
- Those in high tax brackets: Better net yield from good tax-free issues
- Others: 1972 range of taxable yield from 5% (U.S. savings bonds) to ~7.5% (high-grade corporates)

**Higher-Yielding Bond Investments**:

**Second-Grade Issues**:
- Can sacrifice quality for higher income return
- Long experience: Ordinary investor wiser to avoid high-yield bonds
- Expose owner to too many individual risks (price declines to actual defaults)
- Bargain opportunities exist but require special study and skill

**Graham's Warning** (from 1965):
"Experience clearly shows unwise to buy bond/preferred lacking adequate safety merely because yield is attractive"

*1964 Example*:
- Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pacific 5% income debenture bonds
- At 68, yielded 7.35%
- Total interest charges earned only 1.5x in 1963 (vs. requirement of 5x for well-protected railroad)

**When Bought at Full Prices**:
- Very great chance of much lower quotations in future
- When bad business comes (or just bad market), these issues highly susceptible to severe sinking spells
- Often interest/dividends suspended or endangered
- Pronounced price weakness even if operating results not bad

*Railroad Income Bonds Example* (1946-47):
- Ten issues selling at 96+ in 1946 (average 102.5)
- By 1947: Low prices averaged only 68 (lost 1/3 of value in short time)
- Peculiarity: Railroads showing better earnings in 1947 than 1946
- Price decline counter to business picture, reflected general market selloff
- Shrinkage proportionately larger than DJIA common stocks (~23%)

**Graham's Analysis**:
Purchaser at cost above 100:
- Could not expect to participate in further rise
- Only attractive feature: ~4.25% income yield (vs. 2.5% first-grade bonds)
- Advantage of 1.75% in annual income
- But sequel showed risking substantial principal loss for minor income advantage

**The "Businessman's Investment" Fallacy**:
Popular fallacy: Purchase security with larger yield than high-grade issue and accept correspondingly greater risk

Graham's View:
"Bad business to accept acknowledged possibility of principal loss in exchange for mere 1-2% additional yearly income"

*If Willing to Assume Risk*:
Should be certain can realize really substantial gain in principal if things go well

*Conclusion*:
Second-grade 5.5-6% bond selling at par almost always bad purchase
Same issue at 70 might make more sense
"If patient, probably able to buy it at that level"

**Contradictory Attributes of Second-Grade Bonds/Preferreds**:
1. Nearly all suffer severe sinking spells in bad markets
2. Large proportion recover position when favorable conditions return, "work out all right"
3. True even of cumulative preferred stocks that fail to pay dividends for years

*Examples* (Early 1940s):
- Many issues with large dividend accumulations from 1930s depression
- Postwar boom (1945-47): Many accumulations paid off (cash or new securities)
- Principal often discharged as well
- Result: Large profits for buyers when issues friendless and at low prices

**Overall Accounting Question**:
Might higher yields on second-grade issues over time offset irrecoverable principal losses?
- Investor buying all such issues at offering prices might fare as well (or better) than one limited to first-quality
- In long run, possibly

**But For Practical Purposes, Question Largely Irrelevant**:

*Problems*:
1. Buyer at full prices will be worried and discommoded when prices decline precipitously
2. Cannot buy enough issues to assure "average" result
3. Not in position to set aside portion of larger income to offset/amortize permanent losses
4. Common sense: Abstain from buying at 100 if long experience shows can buy at 70 or less in next weak market

**Foreign Government Bonds**:

**Historical Record**:
All investors with even small experience know foreign bonds had bad investment history since 1914
- Inevitable given two world wars and intervening world depression

**Yet Pattern Repeats**:
Every few years, market conditions permit sale of new foreign issues at about par
- Tells us about working of average investor's mind

**Graham's View**:
- No concrete reason to be concerned about well-regarded bonds (Australia, Norway)
- BUT: If/when trouble comes, owner has no legal or other means of enforcing claim

**Examples of Disasters**:
- Republic of Cuba 4.5% sold as high as 117 (1953)
  - Defaulted, sold as low as 20 cents on dollar (1963)
- 1963 NYSE list also included:
  - Belgian Congo 5.25% at 36
  - Greek 7s at 30
  - Various Poland issues as low as 7
- Czechoslovakia 8% bonds (offered 1922 at 96.5):
  - 112 (1928), 67.75 (1932), 106 (1936)
  - 6 (1939), 117 (1946), 35 (1948), 8 (1970)

**Graham's Advice**:
Investor would benefit both country and himself by declining these "opportunities"

**New Issues Generally**:

**Graham's One Recommendation**:
All investors should be **wary** of new issues - subject to:
- Careful examination
- Unusually severe tests before purchase

**Two Reasons for Double Caveat**:
1. New issues have special salesmanship behind them (requires special sales resistance)
2. Most sold under "favorable market conditions" (favorable for seller, therefore less favorable for buyer)

**Effect Increases Down Quality Scale**:
From highest-quality bonds → Second-grade senior issues → Common-stock flotations at bottom

**High-Grade Bond Refinancing**:
- Tremendous amount: Repayment of existing bonds at call price, replacement with lower coupons
- Mostly high-grade bonds and preferred stocks
- Buyers largely financial institutions (qualified to protect interests)
- Carefully priced to meet going rate for comparable issues
- High-powered salesmanship had little effect
- Eventually buyers paid too high a price (many later declined)
- Unpleasant rather than serious for first-quality issue purchasers

**Lower-Grade Issues** (1945-46, 1960-61):

**Different Situation**:
- Selling effort more apparent
- Probably placed with individual and inexpert investors

**Characteristics**:
- Did not make adequate showing based on performance over sufficient years
- Looked safe enough IF recent earnings continued without serious setback
- Investment bankers presumably accepted this assumption
- Salesmen had little difficulty persuading customers likewise
- Nevertheless: Unsound approach to investment, likely to prove costly

**Bull-Market Common Stock Flotations**:

**Pattern**:
Bull markets usually characterized by transformation of privately owned businesses into companies with quoted shares

*Historical Examples*:
- 1945-46
- 1960-on: Reached extraordinary proportions until catastrophic close (May 1962)
- After "swearing-off" period, whole tragicomedy repeated step-by-step (1967-1969)

**New Common-Stock Offerings** (Reproduced unchanged from 1959):

**Two Different Forms**:

### Form 1: Listed Companies Adding Shares
- Additional shares offered pro rata to existing stockholders
- Subscription price set below current market
- "Rights" to subscribe have initial money value
- Sale almost always underwritten by investment banks
- General hope/expectation: All shares taken by exercise of subscription rights
- Does not ordinarily call for active selling effort

### Form 2: Private Companies Going Public
- Most stock sold for account of controlling interests
- Enable them to cash in on favorable market and diversify finances
- When new money raised, often via preferred stock sale

**The Dangerous Pattern**:

*Character of Businesses*:
- Early century: Large proportion of leading companies introduced to public
- Over time: Number of first-rank closely held enterprises steadily diminished
- Original stock flotations concentrated more on relatively small concerns

*Public Prejudice*:
- Simultaneously developed preference for major companies, prejudice against minor ones
- Prejudice weakens as bull markets built up
- Large, quick profits dull critical faculty, sharpen acquisitive instinct

*Quality of Available Private Companies*:
- During bull markets, quite a few showing excellent recent results
- Most would not present impressive record if figures carried back 10+ years

**The Inevitable Sequence**:

*Mid-Bull Market*:
- First common-stock flotations make appearance
- Priced not unattractively
- Some large profits made by early issue buyers

*As Market Rise Continues*:
- Financing grows more frequent
- Quality of companies steadily poorer
- Prices asked/obtained verge on exorbitant

*Dependable Warning Sign of Approaching End*:
New common stocks of small, nondescript companies offered at prices somewhat higher than current level for many medium-sized companies with long market history

*Banking House Quality*:
Very little of this financing done by houses of prime size and reputation

**The Inevitable Result**:

*Collapse*:
Public heedlessness + Selling organizations' willingness to sell anything profitable = Price collapse
Many cases: New issues lose 75%+ of offering price

*Worsening Factor*:
At bottom, public has real aversion to very kind of small issue bought so readily in careless moments
- Many fall, proportionately, as much below true value as formerly sold above it

**Advice for Intelligent Investor**:

*Elementary Requirement*:
Ability to resist blandishments of salesmen offering new common-stock issues during bull markets

*Even If*:
One or two can be found passing severe tests of quality and value, probably bad policy to get mixed up in this business

*Salesman's Response*:
Will point to many issues with good-sized market advances (including spectacular same-day gains)

*Graham's View*:
"All this is part of the speculative atmosphere. It is easy money. For every dollar you make this way you will be lucky if you end up losing only two."

**When to Buy These Issues**:
"Some may prove excellent buys—a few years later, when nobody wants them and they can be had at small fraction of true worth"

**Recent Examples**:

*AAA Enterprises* (First company in S&P Stock Guide):
- Sold to public at $14 (1968)
- Promptly advanced to $28
- Early 1971: Quoted at dismal $0.25
- Even this price gross overvaluation (entered bankruptcy in hopeless condition)
- Story reserved for detailed treatment in Chapter 17

*Aetna Maintenance Co.* (Cited in 1965 edition as "Horrible Example"):
- Sold at $9 (November 1961)
- Promptly advanced to $15
- Fell to $2.375 (next year), to $0.875 (1964)
- Later history "extraordinary" and illustrates "strange metamorphoses in American business"
- Details in Appendix 5

**Final Wisdom**:
Pattern repeats with each generation of investors. Graham's warnings from 1959 remained exactly applicable in 1972—and remain so today.

## Chapter 5: The Defensive Investor and Common Stocks

**Investment Merits of Common Stocks**:

**Historical Context** (1949 First Edition):
- Had to insert long exposition making case for including stocks in portfolios
- Stocks generally viewed as highly speculative, unsafe
- Had declined from 1946 highs
- Instead of attracting investors via reasonable prices, fall undermined confidence

**1949-1969 Transformation**:
- Big advance made stocks appear safe and profitable at record high levels
- Actually carried considerable risk
- Converse of 1949 situation

**The 1949 Argument for Common Stocks**:

Two main points:
1. Offered considerable protection against dollar erosion from inflation (bonds offered none)
2. Higher average return over years from:
   - Average dividend income exceeding good bond yields
   - Underlying tendency for market value to increase (consequence of reinvested profits)

**Important Caveat**:
Always warned these benefits could be lost if buyer pays too high a price
- Clearly the case in 1929 (took 25 years for market to recover)
- Since 1957, stocks once again lost traditional advantage in dividend yield over bonds

**1972 Analysis**:
Must wait to see if inflation factor and economic-growth factor will make up for significantly adverse development (yield disadvantage)

**Graham's 1972 Position** (DJIA 900):
"We have no enthusiasm for common stocks in general at 900 DJIA"
Yet: "Defensive investor cannot afford to be without appreciable proportion of common stocks in portfolio, even if regard them as lesser of two evils—greater being risks attached to all-bond holding"

**Rules for Common-Stock Component**:

### Rule 1: Adequate Diversification
- Minimum: 10 different issues
- Maximum: About 30 issues
- Adjectives indefinite but general sense clear

### Rule 2: Large, Prominent, Conservatively Financed
- Often referred to as "primary" companies
- All others called "secondary" (except growth stocks placed in separate class)
- To be "large" (1971): $50 million assets or $50 million business
- To be "prominent": Rank among first quarter or third in size within industry

### Rule 3: Long Record of Continuous Dividend Payments
- All DJIA issues met this requirement (1971)
- Specific suggestion: Continuous dividends beginning at least in 1950

### Rule 4: Price Limit Relative to Earnings
- Set limit on price relative to average earnings over, say, past 7 years
- Suggestion: 25x average earnings, not more than 20x last 12-month earnings
- BUT: Would eliminate nearly all strongest, most popular companies
- Would ban virtually entire category of "growth stocks"

**Growth Stocks and the Defensive Investor**:

**Definition**:
Growth stock: Increased per-share earnings in past well above rate for common stocks generally, expected to continue in future
- Some say true growth stock should double per-share earnings in 10 years (7.1%+ annual rate)

**The Attraction**:
Obviously attractive to buy and own, provided price not excessive
**The Problem**:
Always in the price

**Characteristics**:
- Long sold at high prices relative to current earnings
- Much higher multiples of average profits over past period
- Introduced speculative element of considerable weight
- Made successful operations far from simple

**Examples**:

*IBM (Leading Growth Issue)*:
- Brought phenomenal rewards to those who bought years ago and held
- BUT: Lost 50% of market price in six months (1961-62)
- Nearly same percentage loss 1969-70
- Even "best of common stocks" highly vulnerable

*Texas Instruments*:
- Six years: Rose from $5 to $256 (no dividend)
- Earnings increased from $0.40 to $3.91 per share
- Note: Price advanced five times as fast as profits (characteristic of popular stocks)
- Two years later:
  - Earnings dropped nearly 50%
  - Price dropped four-fifths to $49

**Graham's Conclusion for Defensive Investor**:
Growth stocks as a whole too uncertain and risky
- Of course wonders can be accomplished with right selections, bought at right levels, sold after huge rise
- But average investor can no more expect to accomplish this than to find money growing on trees

**Alternative Recommendation**:
Group of large companies that are relatively unpopular, obtainable at reasonable earnings multipliers
- Offers sound if unspectacular area of choice by general public
- Will illustrate this idea in chapter on portfolio selection

**Portfolio Changes**:

**Standard Practice**:
Submit all security lists for periodic inspection to see if quality can be improved

**Service Providers**:
- Investment counselors (major part of service for clients)
- Nearly all brokerage houses (make suggestions without special fee for commission business)
- Some brokerage houses maintain fee-basis investment services

**For Defensive Investor**:
Should obtain—at least once per year—same advice regarding changes as sought when funds first committed

**Critical Points**:
1. Since little expertness of own, essential to entrust only to firms of highest reputation
2. Otherwise may fall into incompetent or unscrupulous hands
3. At every consultation, make clear desire to adhere closely to four rules of common-stock selection

**Note**: If list competently selected initially, should be no need for frequent or numerous changes

**Dollar-Cost Averaging**:

**NYSE "Monthly Purchase Plan"**:
Investor devotes same dollar amount each month to buying one or more common stocks
- Application of "formula investment" known as dollar-cost averaging
- During predominantly rising market since 1949, results certain to be highly satisfactory
- Prevented concentration of buying at wrong times

**Lucile Tomlinson Study**:
Comprehensive study of formula investment plans:
- Calculated results of dollar-cost averaging in DJIA stocks
- Tested 23 ten-year purchase periods (first ending 1929, last 1952)
- Every test showed profit either at close of purchase period or within 5 years thereafter
- Average indicated profit at end of 23 periods: 21.5% (exclusive of dividends)
- In some instances, substantial temporary depreciation in market value

**Tomlinson's Striking Conclusion**:
"No one has yet discovered any other formula for investing which can be used with so much confidence of ultimate success, regardless of what may happen to security prices, as Dollar Cost Averaging"

**Objection Addressed**:
May seem unrealistic in practice (few can have same amount available each year for 20 years)

**Graham's Counter**:
- Objection lost much force in recent years
- Common stocks becoming generally accepted as necessary component of sound savings-investment program
- Systematic, uniform purchases may present no more psychological/financial difficulties than similar continuous payments for:
  - U.S. savings bonds
  - Life insurance
- Should be complementary to these
- Monthly amount may be small, but results after 20+ years can be impressive and important

**The Investor's Personal Situation**:

**Three Example Cases**:

### Case 1: Widow with $200,000
- Problem: Living on income very difficult
- Need for conservatism paramount
- Division about equally between U.S. bonds and first-grade stocks
- Compromise between objectives, corresponds to general prescription for defensive investor
- Stock component may go as high as 75% IF:
  - Psychologically prepared for this decision
  - Can be almost certain not buying at too high level (assuredly NOT the case in early 1972)

*What She Must NOT Do*:
Take speculative chances to "make extra income"
- Trying for profits/high income without necessary equipment for success
- Far better to draw $2,000/year from principal to make ends meet than risk half of it in poorly grounded ventures

### Case 2: Successful Doctor (Mid-career, $100,000 savings + $10,000 yearly)
- Has none of widow's pressures
- Yet choices pretty much the same

*Key Questions*:
Is he willing to take serious interest in business of investment?
- If lacks impulse or flair: Best to accept easy role of defensive investor
- Portfolio division no different from typical widow
- Same area of personal choice in stock component size
- Annual savings invested in about same proportions as total fund

*Handicap vs. Widow*:
- More likely to elect enterprising investor role
- Perhaps more likely to succeed
- BUT: Less time available for investment education and fund administration
- Medical men notoriously unsuccessful in security dealings

*Why Doctors Fail*:
- Usually have ample confidence in own intelligence
- Strong desire for good return on money
- Without realization that success requires both:
  - Considerable attention to matter
  - Something of professional approach to security values

### Case 3: Young Man ($200/week, saving $1,000/year)
- Finds himself with same choices (for different reasons)
- Some savings should go automatically into Series E bonds
- Balance so modest seems hardly worthwhile to undergo tough educational/temperamental discipline to qualify as aggressive investor
- Thus simple resort to standard defensive program easiest and most logical policy

*But Human Nature*:
- Finance has fascination for many bright young people with limited means
- Would like to be both intelligent and enterprising with savings
- Even though investment income much less important than salary
- This attitude is all to the good
- Great advantage to begin financial education and experience early
- If going to operate as aggressive investor, certain to make mistakes and take losses
- Youth can stand these disappointments and profit by them

**Graham's Urging**:
Beginner should not waste efforts/money trying to beat market
- Let him study security values
- Initially test judgment on price vs. value with smallest possible sums

**Final Statement**:
"Kind of securities to be purchased and rate of return to be sought depend not on investor's financial resources but on his financial equipment in terms of knowledge, experience, and temperament"

**Note on the Concept of "Risk"**:

**Conventional Usage**:
- Good bonds less risky than good preferred stocks
- Preferred stocks less risky than good common stocks
- From this derived popular prejudice against common stocks as "not safe"
- Demonstrated in Federal Reserve Board's 1948 survey

**Confusion in Word Usage**:
Words "risk" and "safety" applied to securities in two different senses

**Clear Cases of "Unsafe"**:
- Bond that defaults interest or principal payments
- Preferred/common stock bought expecting given dividend rate, then dividend reduced or passed
- Investment with fair possibility holder may have to sell at time when price well below cost

**Extended Usage Problem**:
Idea of risk often extended to apply to possible decline in price:
- Even though decline may be cyclical and temporary
- Even though holder unlikely to be forced to sell

**These Chances Present**:
In all securities (other than U.S. savings bonds)
- Greater extent in general run of common stocks than in senior issues

**Graham's Argument**:
This is not a true risk in useful sense of term

**Analogy**:
Man holding mortgage on building might take substantial loss if forced to sell at unfavorable time
- That element not taken into account in judging safety/risk of ordinary real-estate mortgages
- Only criterion: Certainty of punctual payments

Similarly:
- Risk attached to ordinary commercial business measured by chance of losing money
- Not by what would happen if owner forced to sell

**Graham's Core Conviction** (from Chapter 8):
Bona fide investor does not lose money merely because market price of holdings declines
- Hence fact decline may occur does not mean running true risk of loss
- If group of well-selected common stocks shows satisfactory overall return (measured through fair number of years), then group investment proved to be "safe"
- During that period market value bound to fluctuate, likely as not will sell for while under buyer's cost
- If that makes investment "risky," would have to be called both risky and safe at same time

**Graham's Definition of Risk**:
Apply concept of risk solely to loss of value which either:
- Is realized through actual sale, OR
- Is caused by significant deterioration in company's position, OR
- More frequently perhaps, is result of payment of excessive price in relation to intrinsic worth of security

**Risk in Common Stocks**:
Many common stocks do involve risks of deterioration

**Graham's Thesis**:
Properly executed group investment in common stocks:
- Does not carry substantial risk of this sort
- Therefore should not be termed "risky" merely because of price fluctuation element

BUT such risk IS present if:
- Danger price may prove to have been clearly too high by intrinsic-value standards
- Even if subsequent severe market decline may be recouped many years later

**Note on "Large, Prominent, and Conservatively Financed Corporations"**:

**Financial Conservatism Standard**:
Industrial company's finances not conservative unless:
- Common stock (at book value) represents at least half of total capitalization (including all bank debt)
- For railroad or public utility: At least 30%

**"Large" and "Prominent"**:
- Carry notion of substantial size combined with leading position in industry
- Often referred to as "primary" companies
- All other common stocks called "secondary" (except growth stocks in separate class)

**Concreteness Guidelines** (1972):
- To be "large": $50 million of assets or $50 million of business
- To be "prominent": Rank among first quarter or first third in size within industry group

**Graham's Qualifier**:
Foolish to insist upon such arbitrary criteria
- Offered merely as guides for those who ask for guidance
- Any rule investor sets for himself not violating common-sense meanings of "large" and "prominent" should be acceptable
- By nature of case, large group of companies some will and others won't include among suitable for defensive investment
- No harm in such diversity
- Has salutary effect on stock-market conditions
- Permits gradual differentiation/transition between categories of primary and secondary stock issues

## Chapter 6: Portfolio Policy for Enterprising Investor - Negative Approach

**Base Starting Point**: Same as defensive investor - division between high-grade bonds and high-grade stocks bought at reasonable prices

**Differences**:
Prepared to branch out but wants well-reasoned justification for each departure

**Difficulty**:
No single or ideal pattern - field of choice is wide, depends on:
- Individual's competence and equipment
- Interests and preferences

**Most Useful Generalizations Are Negative**:

### What to Leave Alone:
1. **High-grade preferred stocks** - leave to corporate buyers
2. **Inferior bonds and preferred stocks** - unless at bargain levels (typically 30%+ under par for high-coupon issues, much less for lower coupons)
3. **Foreign-government bond issues** - even if yield attractive
4. **All kinds of new issues**:
   - Convertible bonds and preferreds (even if seem tempting)
   - Common stocks with excellent recent earnings only

**Standard Bond Investment**:
Follow same pattern as defensive investor:
- Choice between high-grade taxable issues (yielding ~7.25% in 1971)
- Good-quality tax-free bonds (yielding up to 5.30% on longer maturities)

**Second-Grade Bonds and Preferred Stocks**:

**Current Situation** (Late 1971):
- Can find first-rate corporate bonds yielding 7.25%+
- Doesn't make sense to buy second-grade merely for higher return
- Poor credit corporations virtually unable to sell "straight bonds" (non-convertibles) to public past 2 years
- Debt financing done via convertible bonds or bonds with warrants

**Implication**:
- Virtually all nonconvertible bonds of inferior rating are older issues selling at large discount
- Offer possibility of substantial principal gain under favorable conditions:
  - Combination of improved credit rating AND lower general interest rates

**Competition from Good Bonds**:
Even in price discounts and chance of principal gain, second-grade bonds compete with better issues

*Examples* (1970):
Well-entrenched obligations with "old-style" coupon rates (2.5-4%) sold at ~50 cents on dollar:
- AT&T 2-5/8% due 1986: Sold at 51
- Atchison Topeka & Santa Fe RR 4% due 1995: Sold at 51
- McGraw-Hill 3-7/8% due 1992: Sold at 50.5

**Conclusion** (Late 1971):
Enterprising investor can probably get from good-grade bonds selling at large discount all he should reasonably desire in both:
- Income
- Chance of appreciation

**Historical Context** (From 1965 Edition):

When high-grade bonds yielded only 4.5%:

*Example*:
Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pacific 5% income debentures:
- Early 1964 at 68, yielded 7.35%
- Total interest charges earned only 1.5x in 1963
- Compare to Graham's requirement of 5x coverage for well-protected railroad

**The Danger of Buying for Yield Alone**:

Experience clearly shows unwise to buy bond/preferred lacking adequate safety merely because yield is attractive (where "merely" implies not selling at large discount, thus not offering substantial principal gain opportunity)

**When Bought at Full Prices** (not many points under 100):
Chances very great holder will see much lower quotations in future because:
- When bad business or just bad market comes
- Issues of this kind prove highly susceptible to severe sinking spells
- Often interest/dividends suspended or endangered
- Frequently pronounced price weakness even though operating results not bad

**Historical Example - Railroad Income Bonds** (1946-47):

*Ten Bonds Study*:
- All sold at 96+ in 1946 (high prices averaging 102.5)
- By following year registered low prices averaging only 68
- Loss of 1/3 market value in very short time

*Peculiarity*:
- Railroads showing much better earnings in 1947 than 1946
- Drastic price decline ran counter to business picture
- Reflection of selloff in general market

*Comparison*:
- Shrinkage proportionately larger than common stocks in DJIA (~23%)
- Purchaser at cost above 100 could not expect to participate in further rise
- Only attractive feature: Income yield averaging ~4.25% (vs. 2.50% for first-grade bonds)
- Advantage of 1.75% in annual income

*Sequel*:
All too soon and plainly showed for minor advantage in annual income, buyer risking loss of substantial part of principal

**The "Businessman's Investment" Fallacy**:

**Popular Concept**:
Purchase security showing larger yield than high-grade issue, accept correspondingly greater risk

**Graham's View**:
"Bad business to accept acknowledged possibility of principal loss in exchange for mere 1-2% additional yearly income"

**If Willing to Assume Risk**:
Should be certain can realize really substantial gain in principal value if things go well

**Conclusion**:
Second-grade 5.5-6% bond selling at par almost always bad purchase
- Same issue at 70 might make more sense
- "If you are patient you will probably be able to buy it at that level"

**Contradictory Attributes**:

Second-grade bonds and preferred stocks possess two contradictory attributes intelligent investor must bear clearly in mind:

1. **Nearly All Suffer Severe Sinking Spells in Bad Markets**
2. **Large Proportion Recover Position When Favorable Conditions Return** - ultimately "work out all right"

*True Even*:
Of cumulative preferred stocks failing to pay dividends for many years

*Historical Example* (Early 1940s):
- Number of such issues from long depression of 1930s
- During postwar boom (1945-47):
  - Many large accumulations paid off (cash or new securities)
  - Principal often discharged as well
- Result: Large profits for people who bought when issues friendless, sold at low prices

**Long-Run Question**:
Might higher yields obtainable on second-grade senior issues prove to have offset irrecoverable principal losses in overall accounting?

In other words:
- Investor buying ALL such issues at offering prices
- Might conceivably fare as well in long run as one limited to first-quality
- Or even somewhat better

**But For Practical Purposes, Question Largely Irrelevant**:

*Reasons*:
1. Regardless of outcome, buyer at full prices will be worried and discommoded when prices decline precipitously
2. Cannot buy enough issues to assure "average" result
3. Not in position to set aside portion of larger income to offset/amortize permanent principal losses
4. Mere common sense: Abstain from buying at ~100 if long experience indicates can probably buy at 70 or less in next weak market

**Historical Reality**:
All investors with even small experience know foreign bonds had bad investment history since 1914

**Yet Pattern Repeats**:
Every few years market conditions permit sale of some new foreign issues at about par

**Graham's View**:
No concrete reason to be concerned about future of well-regarded bonds (Australia, Norway)

- Owner of foreign obligations has no legal or other means of enforcing claim

*Republic of Cuba 4.5%*:

*1963 NYSE Bond List Also Included*:
- Belgian Congo 5.25% at 36

Wild vicissitudes:

- 35 (1948)

Years ago: Rich creditor nation like U.S. under moral obligation to lend abroad

- Time has brought revenge

For many years questioned inherent attractiveness from buyer's standpoint
Perhaps should add now:

**New Issues Generally**:

**Caveat**:
Ill-advised to attempt broad statements about new issues as class (cover widest possible range of quality and attractiveness)

**One Recommendation**:
All investors should be **wary** of new issues - means:
- Should be subjected to careful examination
- Unusually severe tests before purchased

**Two Reasons for Double Caveat**:

1. **Special Salesmanship Behind Them**:
   - Calls for special degree of sales resistance
   - New common stock IPOs sold with 7% "underwriting discount" (built-in commission)
   - Compare to buyer's commission on older shares (typically below 4%)
   - When Wall Street makes roughly 2x as much selling something new vs. old, new gets harder sell

2. **Sold Under "Favorable Market Conditions"**:
   - Favorable for seller, consequently less favorable for buyer
   - Research shows corporations choose to offer new shares when stock market near peak

**Effect Becomes More Important Down Quality Scale**:
From highest-quality bonds → second-grade senior issues → common-stock flotations at bottom

**High-Grade Bond Refinancing**:
- Tremendous amount of repayment of existing bonds at call price
- Replacement by new issues with lower coupons
- Mostly in high-grade bonds and preferred stocks category
- Buyers largely financial institutions (amply qualified to protect interests)
- Carefully priced to meet going rate for comparable issues
- High-powered salesmanship had little effect on outcome
- As interest rates fell lower, buyers finally paid too high price
- Many later declined appreciably in market

*This Is One Aspect*:
General tendency to sell new securities when conditions most favorable to issuer
- But in case of first-quality issues, ill effects to purchaser likely unpleasant rather than serious

**Lower-Grade Issues Different** (1945-46, 1960-61):

*Selling Effort More Apparent*:
- Most issues probably placed with individual and inexpert investors

*Characteristic*:
- Did not make adequate showing judged by company performance over sufficient years
- Did look safe enough IF could assume recent earnings would continue without serious setback

*Process*:
- Investment bankers who brought out issues presumably accepted this assumption
- Salesmen had little difficulty persuading themselves and customers likewise

*Nevertheless*:
- Unsound approach to investment
- Likely to prove costly

**Bull-Market Common-Stock Flotations**:

**Pattern**:
Usually characterized by transformation of privately owned businesses into companies with quoted shares

*Historical Examples*:
- 1945-46
- Again beginning 1960:
  - Process reached extraordinary proportions
  - Catastrophic close May 1962
- After usual "swearing-off" period of several years
- Whole tragicomedy repeated step by step 1967-1969

**New Common-Stock Offerings** (From 1959 Edition):

**Two Forms**:

### Form 1: Listed Companies - Additional Shares
- Offered pro rata to existing stockholders
- Subscription price set below current market
- "Rights" to subscribe have initial money value
- Sale almost always underwritten by investment banks
- General hope/expectation: All shares taken by exercise of subscription rights
- Does not ordinarily call for active selling effort by distributing firms

### Form 2: Private to Public - The Dangerous Pattern

*What's Sold*:
- Most stock sold for account of controlling interests
- Enable them to cash in on favorable market
- Diversify their own finances
- When new money raised for business, often via preferred stock sale

**The Inevitable Cycle**:

*Early Century*:
- Large proportion of leading companies introduced to public trading

*Over Time*:
- Number of first-rank closely held enterprises steadily diminished
- Original common-stock flotations concentrated more on relatively small concerns

*Unfortunate Correlation*:
- Same period: Stock-buying public developing ingrained preference for major companies, prejudice against minor ones

*Bull Market Effect*:
- Prejudice becomes weaker as bull markets built up
- Large, quick profits shown by common stocks as whole sufficient to:
  - Dull public's critical faculty
  - Sharpen acquisitive instinct

*During These Periods*:
Can find quite a number of privately owned concerns enjoying excellent results
- Though most would not present impressive record if figures carried back 10+ years

**When Factors Combined, Following Consequences Emerge**:

### Early Stage:
*Somewhere in middle of bull market*:
- First common-stock flotations make appearance
- Priced not unattractively
- Some large profits made by early issue buyers

### Intensification:
*As market rise continues*:
- This brand of financing grows more frequent
- Quality of companies becomes steadily poorer
- Prices asked and obtained verge on exorbitant

### Warning Sign:
*One fairly dependable sign of approaching end*:
- New common stocks of small, nondescript companies offered at prices somewhat higher than current level for many medium-sized companies with long market history

### Note on Banking Houses:
Should be added that very little of this common-stock financing ordinarily done by banking houses of prime size and reputation
- In Graham's day, most prestigious investment banks steered clear of IPO business (regarded as undignified exploitation)
- By 1999-2000 peak, Wall Street's biggest banks jumped in with both feet
- Cast off traditional prudence, scrambled to foist overvalued stocks on eager public

**The Inevitable Result**:

**Collapse**:
Heedlessness of public + Willingness of selling organizations to sell whatever profitable = Only one result: Price collapse

**Extent of Damage**:
- Many cases: New issues lose 75%+ of offering price

**Worsening Factor**:
- At bottom, public has real aversion to very kind of small issue bought so readily in careless moments
- Many issues fall, proportionately, as much below true value as formerly sold above it

**Requirement for Intelligent Investor**:

**Elementary Ability**:
Resist blandishments of salesmen offering new common-stock issues during bull markets

**Even If**:
One or two can be found passing severe tests of quality and value
- Probably bad policy to get mixed up in this business
